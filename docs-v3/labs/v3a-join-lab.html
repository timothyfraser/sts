LC 01 · observe

With a left join of flights to airlines (the baseline), how many result rows have an NA carrier name?

Read "NA cells" in the sidebar and look for glowing cells in the result. Which table's rows does a left join keep?
0. All 12 sampled flights fly AA, B6, DL, UA or WN, and every one of those carriers is in airlines, so each flight finds its name. The two airlines with no flight here (HA, OO) only appear with a right or full join, which adds 2 rows and 6 NA cells.

LC 02 · intervene

Tick "duplicate a key in y" (UA now appears twice in airlines). What happens to the row count of the left join, and what does R say?

Count the UA flights on the left, then count the lines each one now sends to the right.
Rows go from 12 to 14: each of the 2 UA flights matches 2 UA rows, so each is copied. Keys now repeat on both sides (m:m), so dplyr warns "Detected an unexpected many-to-many relationship between `x` and `y`." It does not stop: the warning is the only signal that the table exploded.

LC 03 · transfer

When would you choose anti_join() over left_join() followed by filter(is.na(...))?

Switch to flights × airports, pick anti_join and tick "blank a matched name in y" (MCO's name becomes NA). Compare "result rows" with "left_join rows, y name NA": the key did match, yet the name is missing.
anti_join() answers "which keys have no partner?" and returns only x's columns, one row per unmatched x row: here 1 flight (to SJU, which is not in airports). left_join() + filter(is.na(name)) answers "which rows lack a name?": with MCO's name blanked it returns 3 rows, the SJU flight plus the 2 MCO flights that matched a row whose name is NA. It also repeats rows when keys are duplicated.
