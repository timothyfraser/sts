Straight line vs street network: what can people reach?

Learning objective: Contrast Euclidean buffers with network-distance isochrones and measure accessibility as population reached.

LC 01 · observe

From Union Square with a 10-minute budget at 25 km/h, how many people does the buffer claim, and how many can the street network actually reach?

Read "people, buffer" and "people, network" in the sidebar at baseline.
The buffer claims 521,671 people; the network reaches 417,128. A straight line is never longer than the street path, so the buffer can only claim more, here 25.1 % more.

LC 02 · intervene

Move the origin to the Williamsburg waterfront, across the East River. Why does the gap between buffer and isochrone grow?

Look at where the hatched block groups sit, and which side of the river they are on.
The buffer claims 689,430 people, the network 392,978: 75.4 % overstated. The hatched area is mostly across the river, which a straight line crosses for free and a street path can only cross at a bridge.

LC 03 · transfer

When is a Euclidean buffer an acceptable stand-in for an accessibility metric?

Compare "% overstated" for Union Square (inland grid) and Long Island City (waterfront).
A buffer is a fair first pass where streets form a fine grid in every direction. Barriers such as water break that: from Long Island City the buffer overstates reach by more than double. Use network distance whenever a barrier or a sparse network sits inside the radius.
