Chapter 8 · rates and uncertainty by area

Small numbers, big swings

Learning objective. Explain why rates in small areas are noisy, attach intervals to area estimates, and see shrinkage toward the mean.

The areas here are 73 one-kilometre grid cells covering Boston, not block groups: the census table in this data is keyed to the grid, so each cell carries its own population.

LC 01 · observe

At baseline, which cell has the highest raw rate, and what is its population?

Read "top cell" and "its population" in the sidebar, or the first row of the table.
Cell 149: 2 sites for 71 residents is 28.2 per 1,000, about 13 times the city rate of 2.14. Two sites is a tiny count, so its 95% interval runs from 3.4 to 101.8.

LC 02 · intervene

Turn on "Shrunk rate". Where does Cell 149 move in the ranking, and why?

Watch "raw leader's rank" and the blue point in the funnel. How far does a point move compared with a large cell?
It drops from 1st to 9th. The shrink weight is A / (A + m / pop): with pop = 71 the weight is small, so its estimate moves most of the way to the city rate, while Cell 93 (1,467 residents) keeps more of its own rate and becomes the leader at 4.6.

LC 03 · transfer

A health department ranks 300 towns by overdose rate. Why is a funnel plot a better first screen than the ranked table?

The limits in the funnel are wide on the left and narrow on the right. What does that say about a rate from a small population?
A ranked table puts the noisiest (smallest) areas at the top and bottom by chance. The funnel's limits widen as population falls, so here only 4 of 61 cells sit above the 99.8% limit, and those are the ones worth a closer look.
